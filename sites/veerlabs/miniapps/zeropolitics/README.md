# Zero Politics

## A Governance Operating System for Post-Party Democracy

This repository contains the working manuscript for Zero Politics, a book-length argument for redesigning democracy beyond permanent party dominance and toward a more citizen-centered, transparent, and competency-driven model of governance.

The canonical book identity used across this repository is:

- Title: Zero Politics
- Subtitle: A Governance Operating System for Post-Party Democracy

## Repository layout

- `chapters/` contains the manuscript in modular Markdown chapters.
- `chapters/README.md` provides the full chapter-by-chapter manuscript map.
- `diagrams/` is reserved for visual system diagrams and supporting figure assets.
- `diagrams/cover/ZeroPolitics_Cover_Front.png` is the current front cover asset used for EPUB and PDF export.
- `diagrams/cover/ZeroPolitics_Civer_Back1.png` is the current back cover asset appended when present.
- `Contents` is the compact working contents list.

## Core themes

The manuscript currently emphasizes these major lines of argument:

1. Politics should be time-bounded rather than allowed to consume public life continuously; elections authorize, and governance should then proceed as disciplined institutional work.
2. Price fairness matters in both directions: preventing exploitative spikes in essential goods while correcting chronic underpayment in farming, manual labor, dairy, and other foundational sectors.
3. Everyday malpractice such as food adulteration and routine cheating should be treated as a central governance failure rather than a minor side issue.
4. Public systems should distinguish legality from legitimacy and introduce stronger ethical scrutiny where technically lawful behavior still damages democratic trust.
5. Judicial and legal reform should be addressed carefully through the lens of access, neutrality, clarity, and stronger constitutional trust.
6. Federal structure should be examined in terms of role clarity, duplication reduction, subsidiarity, and administrative rationalisation without careless attacks on constitutional offices.
7. Compensation justice should recognize dignity, hardship, inconvenience, and public necessity rather than merely status, credential scarcity, or inherited privilege.
8. Media should be treated as democratic infrastructure with responsibilities of clarity, scrutiny, and public trust.
9. Citizens themselves must remain inside the moral argument, because democratic repair cannot be outsourced entirely to institutions.
10. Public communication should become plain, direct, and contestable so citizens do not have to read between the lines of state action.
11. No democratic power should be treated as absolute; every office and mandate must remain time-bounded, reviewable, and constitutionally limited.
12. Government office, government employment, and access to government property should be understood as public trusts rather than social privilege, with strict skepticism toward unnecessary official perks and discretionary use of state facilities.
13. A healthier republic should move away from continuous celebratory political culture, including reflexive guest-list politics, symbolic attendance at every public event, and ritual deference in civic spaces such as schools and local institutions.
14. The relationship between elected representatives and bureaucracy should remain professional rather than feudal or ceremonial, and public money should not be casually wasted on hospitality, spectacle, or routine official feasting.
